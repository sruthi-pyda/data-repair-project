Risk-aware data repair · VIT Vellore

Knowing when not to trust a fix

A spreadsheet has errors in it. Finding them is only half the job — the other half is deciding what to do about each one when you are not sure you are right.

The claim: a calibrated probability of being correct, combined with the explicit cost of being wrong, produces better decisions than acting on raw model confidence. The same confidence should lead to different actions when the consequences change.

The problem

Rare and wrong look identical

Every detector starts from the same instinct: unusual values are suspicious. That instinct is the trap. Both cells below appear exactly once in their column, so nothing about their rarity separates them.

Same column, same frequency, far from every other value.
Correct — just uncommon

A rarity-based detector flags both or neither. Our text detector separates them by asking a different question: is this value suspiciously close to a common one? A typo sits near a frequent value without equalling it; a legitimately rare value sits far from everything. Measured similarity to the nearest frequent value: 0.80+ for the typo, under 0.60 for the rare city.

This is the distinction five earlier attempts in this project failed to make. The section at the end lists them and why each one failed.

What failed

Five attempts, one cause

Picking which column to suspect never worked. Five mechanisms were built, pre-registered with conditions that could fail them, and each one did. They failed for the same underlying reason, and finding that is the more useful result.

AttemptWhat it accidentally measured instead
Likelihood ratiohow predictable the column is in general
Self-supervised injectionhow many distinct values the column has
Contamination fractionhow hard the column is to model
Contamination mixturethe model's own error rate
Excess surprisethe model's own miscalibration

Each fix removed one nuisance and uncovered the next. At these error rates — as low as 1 cell in 400 — the corruption signal sits underneath all of them. And measuring what perfect column selection would even be worth showed it matters on one dataset out of eleven, because every other one has corruption in most of its columns.

These are in the write-up as findings, not omissions. A detector that cannot separate rare from wrong at 0.25% prevalence is a measured limit with an identified mechanism, which is more useful to report than a tuned number.
