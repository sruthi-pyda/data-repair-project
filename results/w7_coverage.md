# W7.0a — detector coverage: which columns can we form an opinion about?

Generated by `experiments/w7_coverage.py`. Seed 42, row cap 8,000.

`results/w5_cross_domain.md` reports a lift figure per dataset and
concludes the method "generalises to six of nine". It reports **no**
coverage, so a reader cannot tell that the conditional detector scores
**0 of 7** of `flights`' columns. The lift numbers are not wrong —
cross-domain evaluates the **rule** detector while the 0/7 is the
**conditional** detector — but reporting effectiveness without
applicability invites the misreading a reviewer will make.

Two notions of coverage, kept separate because they differ:

- **conditional** — columns it does not *explicitly skip*
  (`id_like_cardinality`, `high_cardinality_non_numeric`, `constant`),
  plus any whose score vector is constant anyway. This is the
  applicability limit of the Wave 1 contribution.
- **rule** — columns it ever flags. It has no skip list; it simply
  never fires where none of its six checks apply, which for ranking
  purposes is the same practical limit.

## What this means

The conditional detector's applicability is **severe on text-heavy
tables**, which is what most real dirty-data benchmarks are:

- `flights`: 0/7 columns scored
- `movies`: 2/17 columns scored
- `beers`: 3/11 columns scored
- `rayyan`: 3/11 columns scored

So Wave 1's conditional modelling — the contribution that took adult
from 0.0025 to 0.0120 PR-AUC — **cannot run on most columns of most
of these datasets**. That is a scope limitation and belongs in the
paper as one, not as a footnote.

It also explains the cross-domain pattern without a separate story:
where the rule detector works it is because the corruption is
syntactic, and syntactic corruption is exactly what rules see without
needing a model of the column.
